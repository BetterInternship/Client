“I know I need an internship, but I don't know what I should apply for.”

If your classmates already seem to know exactly where they're applying, that can make the question feel even bigger. But you don't need to have your whole career figured out to take this next step.

Start with what you enjoy doing, what skills you want to practise, and what kind of work you're curious about. We'll narrow it down from there.

You might try a kind of work and realize it isn't for you. That doesn't make the internship a wasted choice. Finding that out is useful too.

> **Quick answer:** Choose a few kinds of work you'd like to try. Read the tasks in each listing, check the important requirements, and compare the schedule and setup. Then browse and apply to roles that are worth exploring, even if you aren't completely sure yet.

## Start with what you actually want to try

Think about your last few school projects. Which parts did you enjoy? Which tasks did you volunteer for without being asked?

Maybe you liked finding patterns in the data. Maybe you preferred designing the slides, building the website, coordinating the group, or explaining the results.

Try answering these three questions:

- What tasks do I enjoy enough to try outside school?
- Which skill would I like more practice with?
- What kind of company or industry am I curious about?

“I'm curious about this” is a good enough answer to start browsing. You don't need a personality test or a five-year plan.

### Match tasks to fields, not just titles

BetterInternship's fields can give you a starting point:

- If you enjoyed working with numbers or building a dashboard, explore **Data & Analytics**. If you liked coding its features, try **Software** too.
- If you're often making pubmats, editing videos, or writing campaign ideas, look at **Creative & Multimedia** or **Marketing**.
- If you like organizing people, schedules, or processes, explore **Operations & Human Resources**.
- If you enjoyed troubleshooting computers or helping classmates use tools, **IT** may be worth a look.
- If you liked financial reports, technical designs, or legal research, explore **Accounting & Finance**, **Engineering & Architecture**, or **Legal**, as appropriate to your background.

These aren't labels you have to keep forever. Pick a couple of fields, read a few listings, and see which tasks interest you.

## Your role doesn't need the same name as your course

An IT student might be interested in analytics. A business student might prefer marketing. A communication student could enjoy HR work. An engineering student might want to explore operations.

So if a role interests you but doesn't have your course name in the title, take a closer look before ruling it out. Read the tasks and see where your skills could fit.

For **credited internships or OJT**, check with your coordinator before committing. Your university or program may have specific rules about eligible tasks, companies, required hours, and work setups.

Requirements vary by school. A role being available on a platform doesn't automatically mean your school will approve it.

## Learn how to read an internship listing

A long list of requirements can look intimidating at first. Slow down and read it in smaller parts. You're checking whether this role could work for you, not looking for a reason to cross yourself out.

### Role title and responsibilities

Ask: **What would I actually do?**

“Marketing Intern” might mean writing content, doing research, organizing events, or helping with reports. Those are different kinds of work.

Look for specific tasks you can picture yourself trying. If the responsibilities are unclear, note what you'd want clarified during the application process or interview.

### Requirements

Separate requirements into three groups:

- **Required:** conditions the company says you need to meet.
- **Preferred:** skills or experience it would like, but may not insist on.
- **Nice to have:** useful extras, especially when described as a bonus.

Look for words like “required,” “preferred,” and “a plus.” If you can't tell which applies, make a note to ask. You don't have to guess, and it's better to clarify than discover a mismatch later.

### Location and work setup

Check whether the role is onsite, hybrid, or remote. For onsite work, check the actual location, not only the city name.

For hybrid work, find out how often you'll need to attend and whether those days are fixed. Remote work may still require specific working hours.

### Schedule and duration

Compare the working days, start date, and internship duration with your classes and school requirements.

If your program requires a particular number of hours, confirm that the proposed arrangement can meet it. Don't assume that a short placement or part-time schedule will be enough.

### Allowance

It's okay for the allowance to matter to you. Transport, meals, and internet cost money. If an allowance is listed, check the amount, when it's paid, and any conditions.

If it isn't listed, don't assume the role is paid or unpaid. Make a note to ask. Include transport, meals, internet, and equipment in your own budget.

### Company

Look at the company's official website and what it actually does. You don't need to spend hours researching before every application.

Check whether the product, services, or industry interest you, and whether the listing gives you enough information about the work. A familiar name isn't a substitute for clear responsibilities.

## Should you apply if you don't meet every requirement?

Read that unfamiliar tool name again. Is it required, or just preferred? Those aren't the same thing.

If the role interests you and you meet the important requirements, it may still be worth applying. You're applying for an internship. You're supposed to still be learning.

For example, a listing might prefer experience with a design tool you've only used in class. You can explain your current level and show the project you made with it.

Some conditions do need to work before you can take the role. Give these a careful check:

- A required course or program.
- Required working days or availability.
- A minimum internship duration.
- Onsite attendance you can't realistically manage.
- Your school's eligibility and approval requirements.

Be honest about your skills. You don't need to call yourself an expert to show that you're ready to learn.

## Choose the work, not only the company name

A well-known company may have a structured internship program. A smaller team may sometimes give interns more direct involvement in projects. Neither is a guarantee.

Compare the particular role. Ask yourself:

- What will I actually work on?
- Will I practise a skill I want to build?
- Who will guide me or give feedback?
- Does the schedule fit my classes?
- Can I manage the commute and costs?
- Would I want to talk about this work in an interview?

It's understandable to be excited about a company you've heard of. Keep that excitement, but also ask what your actual week there would look like.

## Remote, hybrid, or onsite?

There isn't one setup that is best for every student. Choose what is realistic for you and permitted by your school if you're doing credited OJT.

### Remote

Remote work can remove the commute and make location easier to manage. It doesn't necessarily mean flexible working hours.

Consider your internet, workspace, and how comfortable you are asking questions through messages or calls. Ask how interns receive guidance and feedback.

### Hybrid

Hybrid work can offer both home-working days and time with the team. Check how predictable the schedule is.

Can you travel on the required days? What happens if an onsite day conflicts with a class? Don't assume you can choose your own days.

### Onsite

Onsite work can help you see how a workplace operates and ask questions face to face. Some students find it easier to learn around other people.

Think about the commute, transport cost, meals, and time you need before and after work. For Metro Manila travel, compare routes at the hours you'd actually commute.

## Don't make one company your whole plan

It's easy to get attached to one listing when it looks like a good fit.

Save it, apply if it fits, and keep looking. Waiting for one reply can make every day feel longer. Having a few genuine options gives you something else to work on while the company decides.

On [BetterInternship](/search), browse opportunities by field and read the full listings. Sign in to save roles that look promising, compare them, and apply through the platform when you're ready.

Keep track of which roles you've applied to and what happens next. Follow any placement rules your school gives you, and don't accept conflicting commitments.

## When you're stuck between two internships

You don't need a complicated scoring system. Compare five things:

1. Which role gives me more useful experience?
2. Which tasks sound more interesting?
3. Which one fits my schedule better?
4. Which setup is more realistic for me?
5. Which role would I regret not trying?

If you're comparing listings, you can apply to both when appropriate. You don't have to make a final choice before either company has responded.

If these are actual offers, ask about anything still unclear before accepting. You can make a thoughtful choice without knowing how the whole experience will turn out. Your first internship isn't a promise to stay in that field forever.

## When to stop overthinking and apply

You probably won't feel completely sure before every application. That's okay.

If the work interests you, the setup is manageable, and you meet the important requirements, you can take the next step.

For now, choose one useful next step: open BetterInternship, read a few roles, and apply when you find one that makes sense. The rest of your career doesn't need an answer today.
