Got an interview? Take a moment to enjoy that before opening ten tabs of questions.

You don't need to memorize perfect answers. You need to be ready to talk clearly about yourself, what you've worked on, and why you want to learn from the role.

You might still feel nervous after preparing. That doesn't mean the preparation didn't work. Let's get a few things ready so you have something to lean on when your mind goes blank.

> **Quick answer:** Read the role, review your resume, prepare a short introduction and a few project stories, and write down questions for the interviewer. Then check the time, location or link, outfit, and equipment.

## Start with the company and the role

Read the company's official website and the internship description. Write a short answer to these questions:

- What does the company do?
- Which team or kind of work would I join?
- What tasks does the posting mention?
- Which part interests me, and what can I already contribute?

You don't need to recite the company's founding year. Focus on understanding the work and why you'd like to try it. That's a more useful conversation to prepare for.

Check the interview invitation too. Confirm the date, time zone, format, location or link, and anything they've asked you to prepare. If a technical exercise is mentioned, ask about the format if it's unclear.

## Review your own resume

Open the resume you actually sent. It's easy to forget a bullet you wrote weeks ago, especially if you've made several versions. Give yourself a quick refresher before the interview.

For group work, know which part you handled. For a tool you listed, think of a task you actually used it for. If something changed since applying, explain that honestly.

If you're still preparing your application, our [no-experience resume guide](/guides/internship-resume-no-experience) can help you choose clear examples.

## Prepare a few stories, not a script

Pick three situations from school, projects, orgs, volunteering, or work. You can use the same story for more than one question if it's relevant.

For each one, think:

**Situation → Your action → Result → What you learned.**

Maybe your group fell behind on a deadline and you helped sort out the remaining tasks. That's a story. It doesn't need an award or a dramatic ending to show how you work.

Write a few keywords, then practise explaining the story out loud. If the wording changes each time, that's fine. You want to remember what happened, not perform a memorized paragraph.

## Common questions and how to answer them

The examples below are fictional starting points. Change them to match your own experience. Don't memorize them word for word.

### Tell me about yourself.

Think: **Present → Experience → Why you're here.** Aim for roughly 30–60 seconds as a practice target, not a strict rule.

“I'm a third-year IT student at [University]. Most of my experience so far has come from school projects, especially [project]. I enjoyed working on [specific part], which is why I'm interested in getting more hands-on experience through this internship.”

You can stop there and let them ask more. You don't have to fit everything about yourself into the first answer.

### Why do you want this internship?

Think: **Work that interests you → Evidence of that interest → What you want to learn.**

If it's required OJT, you can say so. Then explain why this particular role fits. “I need internship hours” can be true without being your whole answer.

### Why did you apply to our company?

Think: **One thing you found out → Why it connects to you.**

Mention something you've actually read about the product, team, or projects. You don't need to say it's your dream company. A specific, honest reason is enough.

### What are your strengths?

Think: **A relevant strength → One real example.**

Instead of only saying you're organized, explain how you kept a shared task sheet updated during a class project. Choose something that helps with the internship's work.

### What's something you're still improving?

Think: **A genuine skill you're developing → What you're doing about it.**

Maybe you're practising presenting without reading every slide. Say that, then explain what you're trying to improve. You don't need to disguise it as “I work too hard.” Learning something is a normal part of being an intern.

### Tell me about a project you're proud of.

Think: **Goal → Your part → Output → Learning.**

Explain enough context for someone outside your course to understand. If a project wasn't deployed or a campaign was only a class proposal, say that.

### Tell me about a time you worked with a team.

Think: **Shared task → Coordination or difficulty → Your response → Outcome.**

Group work isn't always smooth, so you don't have to pretend it was. Briefly explain the problem, then spend more time on how you responded. Keep your teammates' names out of it.

### How do you handle deadlines?

Think: **How you plan → How you track progress → What you do if you're behind.**

Use a real example. Explain how you prioritised tasks or asked for clarification early. Don't promise you've never missed a deadline if that's not true.

### What do you want to learn during your internship?

Think: **A task or skill from the role → Why it matters to you.**

“I'd like to learn how your team checks software changes before release” is more useful than “I want to learn everything.” You can also ask how interns receive feedback.

### Do you have any questions for us?

Prepare two or three questions. Choose the ones the conversation hasn't already answered:

- What would an intern work on in the first few weeks?
- Who would supervise me, and how do interns receive feedback?
- What would doing well in this role look like?
- What schedule and work setup should I expect?
- Who coordinates school documents if the placement is required OJT?
- What are the next steps after this interview?

It's also reasonable to clarify allowance or equipment when these aren't disclosed. Ask politely rather than assuming the answer.

## When you don't know an answer

You can pause. A few seconds to think can feel much longer to you than to the person asking.

Try “Could I take a moment to think?” or “Could you clarify what you mean by that?” if you need to.

If you don't know a tool or concept, say so. Then explain any related experience or how you'd approach learning it. For a problem-solving question, you can talk through your thinking if the interviewer allows it.

“I haven't used that tool yet. I've worked with [related tool], and I'd start by checking [documentation or a small example]” is an honest response if it's true.

An honest “I haven't learned that yet” gives you a better starting point than trying to bluff your way through. Follow it with what you do know or how you'd start learning it.

## Get the practical things ready

### What to wear

Follow any dress instructions in the invitation. If none are given, clean, neat smart-casual clothing is often a sensible starting point. Expectations vary by company and role.

You don't need to buy an expensive outfit. Choose something comfortable enough that you aren't distracted by it.

### For an online interview

- Test your microphone, camera, and internet beforehand.
- Open the correct meeting link and check your display name.
- Find a quiet place and put the light in front of you if possible.
- Charge your device and silence notifications.
- Keep your resume and a few keyword notes nearby.

If your connection drops, try to reconnect and explain briefly. You don't need to keep apologizing for a technical problem. If you can't rejoin, contact the recruiter through the agreed channel, so keep that contact handy.

### For an onsite interview

Confirm the building, entrance, travel route, and contact person. For Metro Manila commutes, allow a buffer for traffic and security checks.

Bring what's requested. Useful basics can include a resume copy, relevant portfolio, pen, notes, and any ID required by building security. Don't carry every school document unless they asked for it.

Plan to arrive nearby early. About 10–15 minutes before the appointment is a reasonable check-in target unless the company says otherwise. If you're delayed, let the contact know as soon as you can.

## After the interview

Before replaying every answer in your head, write down what you learned. Was the schedule clear? Do the tasks interest you? What happens next?

You were getting to know the company too. The interview isn't only about whether they choose you.

You can send a short thank-you message through the contact channel the recruiter used, mentioning one thing you discussed. If you need a status update later, follow the timeline they gave you.

Then give yourself a break from analysing every sentence you said. You can't know the result yet, and one awkward answer doesn't tell you how the whole interview went.

Keep considering other suitable roles while you wait. If you're unsure where to look next, read [how to find the right internship for you](/guides/how-to-find-the-right-internship).

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Memorizing every answer.** Remember your examples and main points instead.
- **Not knowing your own resume.** Review what you submitted.
- **Giving only vague traits.** Add a real example.
- **Pretending to know a tool.** Explain your actual level.
- **Ignoring the practical details.** A wrong link or unclear location adds avoidable stress.
